# Three conversations before the 27th

Jury question 7 is "have you tested this with a real family?" Today the answer
is no, and it is the only question on the list that cannot be answered by
writing more code. This is how to change that in an afternoon.

You do not need a pilot, a signed institution, or anyone to actually recover
money. You need to have talked to people this is for, and to be able to quote
them. That alone puts you ahead of almost every team in the room.

## Who to call

Pick whichever is easier to reach. One good branch officer conversation is
worth more than three vague family ones.

**Option A, three families.** Anyone whose parent or grandparent has died and
who dealt with the paperwork. Relatives, neighbours, a friend's parents, people
at a housing society. You are looking for someone who has held an old passbook
or an LIC policy and wondered what to do with it.

**Option B, one bank branch officer.** Walk into any SBI, Bank of Baroda or
Union Bank branch and ask for whoever handles **deceased claim settlement**.
That is the internal term; using it gets you past the counter. Ask for ten
minutes. Many will give it, because nobody ever asks them about this.

## What to ask a family

Do not pitch. If you demo first you will only hear politeness. Ask about what
already happened to them, then show the product at the end if there is time.

1. When it came to sorting out the paperwork, what did you actually have to do
   first? Who told you what to do?
2. Was there any money or policy you were not sure about, where you did not
   know whether it was worth chasing?
3. Did anyone offer to help for a fee? What did they ask for?
4. How many separate offices or websites did you end up dealing with?
5. What was the most frustrating single moment?

Then, only at the end: here is what I built, would this have helped, and what
is missing?

## What to ask a branch officer

1. How often does a family come in about an account of someone who has died?
2. What is the most common reason you have to send them away and ask them to
   come back?
3. If a family arrived with a complete, correct set of documents the first
   time, how much time would that save you?

Question 2 is the one that matters. It tells you what the rule corpus is
getting wrong, and the answer is usually specific and surprising.

## What to do with what you hear

**Write down exact words.** A quote is evidence; a paraphrase is an opinion.
Ask "can I write that down and use it, without your name?" Almost everyone
says yes.

Then three places to put it:

- The **problem slide** in the deck, as one line under the statistics. A real
  sentence from a real person next to ₹1.84 lakh crore is worth more than
  another statistic.
- **Question 7** becomes: "Three families, and here is what one of them said."
- Anything that contradicts the product goes on the **honesty list** on the
  judges page, and you say so before the panel finds it.

## If they contradict you

That is the most valuable outcome, not the worst one. If a family says the
hard part was something Virasat does not touch, say that on the 27th. A founder
who changed their mind because they talked to a user is the single most
fundable thing you can look like in that room.

## If you run out of time

Do not invent it and do not imply it. Say plainly: not yet, it is the most
important thing missing, and here is what I built it from instead, which is
documented procedures and public sources, every one cited on the Sources page.
That answer costs you less than a bluff a panel sees through.
